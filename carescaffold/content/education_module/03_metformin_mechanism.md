# Metformin: How It Works in the Body

Metformin is the medication most people with newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes are started on. It's been in use for decades, has a well-understood safety profile, and is usually the first choice when no other reason suggests a different starting point. This document explains what metformin is doing in your body — not how much to take or when to take your specific dose; those are decisions for you and your care team.

The main thing metformin does is reduce the amount of glucose your liver releases into your bloodstream. Your liver makes glucose overnight and between meals to keep your brain and body fueled when you're not eating. In Type 2 diabetes, that process can run a bit too enthusiastically, contributing to higher fasting blood sugars. Metformin dials that back. You can think of it as a dimmer switch on the liver's glucose output rather than an on/off switch.

A second effect: metformin makes your body's cells a bit more responsive to the insulin you already produce. This is sometimes described as improving *insulin sensitivity*. The insulin your pancreas makes continues to work, but metformin helps your muscle and fat cells take up glucose from the bloodstream more readily. It does not push your pancreas to make more insulin, which is one reason it's generally less likely to cause low blood sugar than some other diabetes medications.

A third, smaller effect: metformin slightly slows how quickly your intestines absorb glucose from food, which can soften the post-meal rise in blood sugar. This effect is more modest than the first two.

What metformin does *not* do is also worth knowing:
- It does not cure Type 2 diabetes; it manages it while you take it.
- It does not usually cause weight gain, and sometimes people lose a small amount of weight on it — the opposite of insulin and certain other diabetes medications.
- It does not generally cause low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) on its own, because it doesn't stimulate extra insulin release. Low blood sugar becomes a concern when metformin is combined with other medications (like sulfonylureas) or insulin.
- It does not stress the kidneys in the way many people assume. Metformin is cleared by the kidneys, so kidney function matters for dosing — but the medication itself is not toxic to normal kidneys. Your care team will check your kidney function before prescribing and periodically afterward.

Metformin takes effect gradually over days to a couple of weeks; you won't feel it working the way you'd feel a stimulant or a pain reliever. The way it works is visible in your glucose readings and your A1C over time, not in the moment.

If you have questions about your specific dose, your timing, or whether metformin is right for you, that's a conversation for your care team — this document can't answer those questions. What it can do is help you understand, in plain terms, what the medication is doing in your body so that conversation is more productive.
