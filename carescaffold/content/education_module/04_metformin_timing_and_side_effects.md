# Metformin Timing and Side Effects

This document covers the practical experience of taking metformin — when in the day people typically take it, what side effects are common, and what's worth telling your care team about. It does not tell you what your dose should be or when you specifically should take your medication; those depend on the prescription your clinician wrote for you and how your body responds.

**Timing.** Metformin is most often taken with meals, and the reason is largely about stomach comfort rather than how well the medication works. Taking it on an empty stomach increases the chance of nausea or stomach upset for many people; food in the stomach softens that. Many prescriptions are written for once daily with the evening meal to start, then adjusted based on how you tolerate it. Some people take it twice daily with breakfast and dinner. Sustained-release (also called extended-release or "XR") formulations tend to be gentler on the stomach and are sometimes prescribed once a day.

**Common side effects, especially early.** The most commonly reported side effects are stomach-related:
- Nausea or feeling queasy — usually mild and often improves over the first 1–2 weeks
- Loose stools or diarrhea — often temporary, often improves as your body adjusts
- A metallic taste in the mouth for a short time after taking it
- Mild loss of appetite
- Occasional stomach cramping or bloating

For most people these effects are temporary and fade as the body adjusts. Starting at a low dose and increasing slowly (a common practice) tends to reduce how often they appear. Taking metformin with food, as mentioned above, also helps.

**Less common but worth knowing.** A rare but serious side effect called lactic acidosis is associated with metformin, particularly in people with severe kidney problems, liver disease, heart failure, or conditions that reduce oxygen to tissues. Symptoms include unusual weakness, trouble breathing, stomach pain with vomiting, feeling cold (especially in the arms and legs), dizziness, or a slow or irregular heartbeat. This is a medical emergency; seek immediate care if you experience these. This is rare in people with normal kidney function, but the reason your clinician checks kidney function periodically is to keep that risk low.

**Vitamin B12.** Long-term metformin use can reduce B12 absorption in some people. Symptoms of low B12 include numbness or tingling in hands and feet, fatigue, or memory changes. Many clinicians include a B12 check in routine bloodwork for people who have been on metformin for years. If you notice any of those symptoms, tell your care team — it's a simple blood test and the treatment is straightforward if needed.

**What to tell your care team.** Communicate if:
- Stomach side effects don't improve after 2–3 weeks, or are severe
- You're scheduled for imaging that uses contrast dye (some procedures require you to pause metformin temporarily)
- You have an illness with vomiting, diarrhea, or trouble drinking fluids (dehydration can briefly affect kidney function and how your body handles metformin)
- You're planning to drink alcohol heavily or started a new medication
- You're pregnant or planning to become pregnant — metformin may or may not be the right choice during pregnancy

**A common question: what if I miss a dose?** The general principle for metformin is to take it as soon as you remember, unless it's close to your next scheduled dose — in which case skip the missed one and continue normally. Do not double up to make up for a missed dose; this raises the chance of stomach upset without providing any benefit. Specific guidance for your situation is a question for your pharmacist or prescriber.

This document is about what to expect and what to ask about — not about what you should do. Dose, timing, and any changes are decisions for your care team based on your specific situation.
