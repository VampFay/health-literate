# A1C Target Explained

The A1C test (sometimes called hemoglobin A1C, HbA1c, or glycohemoglobin) is a blood test that estimates your average blood sugar over roughly the past two to three months. It doesn't replace the daily checks you do with a glucose meter; it adds a different kind of information — the long view.

How it works, in plain terms: glucose in your bloodstream slowly attaches to hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The more glucose floating around, the more of it sticks. Red blood cells live about three months on average, so the percentage of hemoglobin with glucose attached reflects your average glucose over that window.

A1C is reported as a percentage. An A1C of 6% corresponds to an estimated average glucose of about 126 mg/dL; 7% to about 154 mg/dL; 8% to about 183 mg/dL; 9% to about 212 mg/dL; 10% to about 240 mg/dL. Each 1% change translates to roughly a 28–29 mg/dL change in estimated average glucose. This means small numerical changes in A1C represent meaningful changes in how much glucose your body has been exposed to over time.

Diagnosis thresholds that doctors commonly use:
- An A1C below 5.7% is considered normal.
- An A1C of 5.7% to 6.4% suggests prediabetes — a warning sign that warrants attention, but not yet diabetes.
- An A1C of 6.5% or higher, when confirmed on a repeat test, is one way diabetes is diagnosed.

For many adults who already have diabetes, a common treatment target is an A1C below 7%. Your individual target may be different — some people benefit from a tighter target (below 6.5%) and others from a less strict target (below 8%) depending on age, other health conditions, and how low blood sugar episodes affect you. Your care team sets your personal target with you; this document does not.

A useful framing: A1C is a trailing indicator, like a fuel-economy gauge on a car. It tells you what happened, not what's happening right now. Daily glucose readings and how you feel moment-to-moment are the real-time dashboard. Both matter; they answer different questions.

Two practical notes. First, A1C can be less accurate for people with certain conditions — anemia, kidney disease, recent blood loss or transfusion, or hemoglobin variants (more common in people of African, Mediterranean, or Southeast Asian heritage). If your A1C result seems to disagree with your daily glucose readings, ask your care team whether one of these could be a factor. Second, a single A1C result is informative but trends across several results are more useful than any one number; a single reading is not a report card.
